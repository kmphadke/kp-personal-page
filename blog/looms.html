The Looms That Sold for $26

On 24 October 2026, Apollo’s chief economist Torsten Slok published a set of charts with a blunt conclusion. In his words, AI has delivered “zero financial payoff to corporate America outside of tech.” Revenue per worker and profit margins are rising for the Magnificent Seven tech firms. For the rest of the S&P 500, which Apollo calls the S&P 493, they are flat.

The natural reading, and the one in Apollo’s note, is that AI is not yet delivering outside tech. Apollo also asks whether firms can turn increasingly cheap, commoditised AI into higher margins, and suggests that may be harder than it sounds. I think a much older story explains why.

Buffett’s Looms

In his 1985 letter to shareholders, Warren Buffett explained why Berkshire Hathaway had closed its textile business. Berkshire had the chance to make large investments that would cut costs. On standard return-on-investment tests, they usually looked attractive. The benefits, he wrote, were illusory.

Competitors made the same kind of investments. Once enough of them did, the lower costs became lower prices across the industry. He compared it to a parade where “each person watching a parade decides he can see a little better if he stands on tiptoes.” At the 1986 auction, looms Berkshire had bought for $5,000 apiece in 1981 found no takers at $50. They went for scrap at $26.

Why Savings Leak Away

When a cost-cutting technology is available to every firm in a competitive industry, the saving does not stay with the firm for long. Early adopters may enjoy a short-lived edge. Once rivals catch up, competition passes much of the gain to customers through lower prices. Profit survives only where something stops rivals copying, such as a scarce asset or a strong network.

Seen this way, flat margins in the S&P 493 are not, on their own, evidence that AI has failed there. If every bank and every retailer can rent the same general-purpose model on similar terms, that model looks more like Buffett’s looms than like a moat. However well it works, competition should hand much of the benefit onward. Apollo also notes that mainstream firms are starting to treat token spending as a cost centre that weighs on margins. That may simply be accurate accounting.

What This Does Not Prove

This lens does not prove that hidden gains exist. If AI were raising productivity and the gains were being passed on, they should eventually show up in productivity across the economy. Apollo’s third chart, which uses the San Francisco Fed’s measure of total factor productivity, shows no sign of that yet. So the honest claim is narrower. Margins are the wrong place to look for the gains from a technology everyone can buy. Prices and economy-wide productivity are better places, and there the evidence is not yet in.

The Cost of Staying in the Parade

For most firms outside tech, I think AI spending should be judged as the cost of staying in the parade, not as an investment that will lift returns. In finance terms it behaves more like maintenance spending than growth investment. Boards that approve it expecting wider margins may be disappointed even if the technology works exactly as promised.

For a leader, the useful question is not whether AI will cut costs. It is whether competitors can buy the same cut. If they can, do it well and expect the benefit to reach customers. Look for returns only where AI deepens something rivals cannot easily copy, such as proprietary data or a long customer relationship.

For students, the same logic applies to careers. A skill anyone can gain with the same tool will be competed down in price. The premium sits with what is harder to copy.

Margins are the wrong place to look for the gains from a technology everyone can buy.

If AI turns out to be mostly a shared cost of staying competitive, which industries do you expect to be the exception, where the gains stay with the firm rather than flowing to customers?

Apollo’s findings are directional statements from its own split of the S&P 500, and this essay uses no values read from its charts. Buffett’s loom prices are nominal and describe a liquidation sale in an industry with excess capacity. The pass-through argument is my interpretation and depends on competition. It does not prove that AI gains exist, only that margins are a poor place to look for them.
