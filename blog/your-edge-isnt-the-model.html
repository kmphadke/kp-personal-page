6 July 2026 · Hiring & the Future of Work

In 2026, Your Edge Isn't the Model. It's Whether You Can Explain It.

Picture this: you're in an interview, confident about your technical skills. You're ready for questions about algorithms and datasets. Instead, you're asked to explain your project to someone who has never seen a model in their life. In that moment, the real test isn't your expertise. It's whether you can turn complexity into clarity.

It's a moment many job seekers are running into in 2026. A hiring manager said it to me bluntly after a campus presentation:

"Your students can model anything. But can they walk a client through what the model means?"

That question has stayed with me ever since. Here's what I've come to believe: technical skill gets you to the interview. The ability to translate it — to make a model mean something to the person across the table — is what gets you hired, and what makes you rise once you're in.

GMAC's 2026 Corporate Recruiters Survey backs this up. It drew on 621 recruiters and hiring managers across 39 countries, the majority of them at Global Fortune 500 companies. AI, data-analysis, and technology skills saw the biggest jump in importance over the past year — no surprise there. Yet the capabilities employers weigh most when they actually hire are communication, problem-solving, and adaptability. And over the next five years, they expect skills in using AI tools, paired with strategic thinking, to matter most. The message isn't AI instead of human skills. It's both, working together.

AI as a multiplier, not a threat

GMAC's CEO, Joy Jones, put it well: "The future belongs to professionals who can use emerging technology as a multiplier rather than view it as a competitive threat." That word — multiplier — is the one I'd ask every young professional reading this to sit with. The human capabilities employers prize — communication, adaptability, judgment — are precisely the ones AI can't easily replicate. AI multiplies what you already bring. If what you bring is the ability to explain, decide, and lead, it multiplies that.

The professionalism signal

The survey also surfaces a quieter concern: professionalism. Employers increasingly flag it as something they don't consistently see in today's graduates, alongside small dips in confidence on core skills like communication. That's a signal, not a verdict. Professionalism isn't one fixed thing — it means something specific in a client-facing role, and something just as specific on a project site or inside a data team. Define it for the room you're in.

What this means if you're early in your career

Here's the practical takeaway. Learn the AI tools — they clearly matter, and their importance is only rising. But invest just as hard in the things no model can copy: communication, resilience, and judgment applied in real situations. The graduates I've watched move fastest, in the corporate world and in my classroom, were rarely the ones who knew the most tools. They were the ones who could explain what the tools were saying — and why it mattered to the person across the table.

Employers remain confident in management education — more than half say a business degree matters more than ever as technology advances. But the bar is rising, and the curriculum has to rise with it.

What's your experience with this? If you're a few years into your career, what's one skill you wish you'd built more deliberately before your first job?

Source: GMAC 2026 Corporate Recruiters Survey. Figures cited are drawn from GMAC's published findings.
