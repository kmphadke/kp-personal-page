What a Company Chooses to Sell

In the first nine months of 2025, Meta spent $26.2 billion buying back its own shares. In the first nine months of 2026, it spent nothing on buybacks. Then, on 26 October 2026, it announced the sale of about $35 billion of new shares to investors, and plans to sell up to $60 billion more gradually on the stock market. Alphabet, Google’s parent company, announced an $80 billion package of share sales of its own in June.

Several headlines focused on how the sale would dilute existing shareholders and protect the founder’s voting control. I think the more useful question is why a company with a top-tier credit rating, one that can borrow cheaply, would choose to sell shares at all.

An Old Answer From Finance

Finance has a precise answer, and it is more than forty years old. In 1984, the economists Stewart Myers and Nicholas Majluf looked at what happens when managers know more about their company than outside investors do. They showed that the way a company raises money becomes a signal in itself.

Their argument runs like this. A company’s first choice is its own cash. Its second choice is safe borrowing, because lenders get repaid whatever managers privately know. Selling new shares comes last. Investors reason that if managers thought their shares were cheap, they would not be selling them, so a share sale makes investors suspicious. In their model, a company that can still borrow safely never sells shares at all. Finance teachers call this ranking the pecking order.

Where Meta Sits on the Ladder

Now look at Meta’s own numbers for July to September. Free cash flow is the cash a business brings in minus what it spends on new investment. Meta’s business brought in $32.8 billion of cash and spent $34.9 billion on new buildings and equipment. That left free cash flow of minus $2.1 billion, in a year when Meta expects to spend $135 to $145 billion in total. In the third quarter, the business no longer covered its own spending.

The second rung is already in use too. Meta’s long-term borrowing rose from $58.7 billion at the end of 2025 to $80.9 billion by the end of September. Now it has reached the third rung, selling shares, while still holding about $82 billion of cash and investments. Myers and Majluf would not find that odd. Their model helps explain why companies like to keep a cash cushion for future opportunities.

What This Does and Does Not Mean

None of this says Meta is making a mistake. Myers and Majluf themselves showed that selling shares to fund a valuable project can be in existing shareholders’ interest. Meta says the sale will widen its sources of funding. Alphabet said about $30 billion of its gradual share sales would go towards tax bills on shares it awards to employees, which is closer to housekeeping than to a statement about value. And the gap between what managers and investors know is probably smaller for companies that analysts follow as closely as these.

But the signal does not disappear because the company is large. Meta’s borrowing is not free of risk, and each new bond adds to its debts. Once borrowing carries risk, the model allows a share sale to make sense. A year earlier, Meta’s spending still left room for $26 billion of buybacks. Now part of it will be paid for by selling a slice of the company to new owners.

I think the order in which a company reaches for money is the most honest disclosure in this investment cycle. Earnings calls tell you what management expects. The financing tells you how far the spending has outrun the cash the business produces. When a company moves from buying its own shares to selling them, I read that as management saying its own cash will not be enough for what comes next. It is not yet a verdict on whether the spending will pay off.

For a board or finance director outside big tech, the practical test has two parts. Ask which rung your own AI budget already sits on. And judge the project against the full cost of the money behind it, not just the interest rate on your cheapest loan. For students, the lesson goes well beyond this story. When you want to know what a company believes, watch what it chooses to sell.

Do you read Meta’s share sale as confidence in its projects, or as a sign that the spending has outgrown the business that pays for it?

Meta’s free cash flow for the first nine months of 2026 was still positive; only the third quarter was negative, so the share sale is best read as funding for spending still to come. Long-term debt rose over the year but fell slightly between June and September, when Meta drew on its cash instead. The $35 billion includes about $12.5 billion of preferred shares that convert into ordinary shares later, and the $60 billion programme of gradual sales had not formally begun when it was announced. The pecking order is one of several theories of how companies finance themselves, and placing Meta on it is my interpretation.
